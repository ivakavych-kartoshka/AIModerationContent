      Abstract. Hate     speech     on   Vietnamese   social   media   is   a   pressing
      moderation problem, and ne-tuned multilingual transformers such
      as mDeBERTa-v3 are the default remedyyet reported gains usually
      bundle several independent design decisions (head, learning-rate sched-
      ule, loss, class weighting, curriculum, decision rule) into one number,
      so it is unclear which decision matters. We present SensitiveAI, a
      controlled ablation of six systems sharing one xed split, label mapping,
      backbone (microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base) and seed, diering only in
      the optimisation recipe, each scored once on the ViHSD test set (three
      classes HATE / OFFENSIVE / CLEAN, 83% CLEAN). All six land in
      a narrow 0.6540.664 macro-F1 and 0.8630.878 accuracy band; no pair-
      wise macro-F1 dierence is signicant under a paired bootstrap, and the
      only signicant paired dierences are in accuracy, where the focal-loss
      probe buys the best HATE F1 (0.609) and macro ROC-AUC (0.893) by
      giving up   clean recall. A multi-view head with discriminative learning
      rates reaches the best macro-F1 (0.6638), but the highest-accuracy
      system has the lowest macro-F1the classic imbalanced-classication
      trapand a three-stage curriculum and a validation-searched threshold
      rule never improved over simpler recipes. We release the full protocol,
      bootstrap condence intervals, a confusion-matrix and error analysis
      and the negative results, so the comparison can be reproduced rather
      than re-argued.

      class imbalance · ablation study · reproducibility.




1    Introduction
Vietnam has one of the fastest-growing social-media user bases in Southeast
Asia, and the volume of Vietnamese text platforms must moderate keeps rising.
Flagging hateful and oensive comments automatically is therefore a practi-
cal prerequisite for healthier communities, yet Vietnamese remains low-resource
and its social-media style is hard (code-switching, teencode, informal spellings,
evasive substitutions, dropped diacritics).
2         P. K. Vy and C. H. Nga



      The release of UIT-ViHSD [11], a human-annotated corpus of roughly 33k
Vietnamese comments labelled clean, offensive or hate, made supervised
transformer ne-tuning a realistic default and a standard benchmark for mono-
lingual encoders such as PhoBERT [13] and multilingual ones such as mBERT
and XLM-R [2,16,7]. Multilingual DeBERTa-v3 variants have become popular
because they combine a large multilingual pre-training corpus with disentangled
attention [6,5].
      In parallel, many optimisation tricks are routinely applied to imbalanced
text classication: a richer classication head, discriminative (layer-wise) learn-
ing rates, multi-stage ne-tuning, class-weighted or focal losses [9], curriculum
learning [1], and per-class decision thresholds [18]. In the literature these are usu-
ally bundled together and reported as a single end-to-end improvement, which
leaves a practical question unanswered: which of these decisions actually moves
the number on Vietnamese hate speech detection, and by how much?
      This paper answers that with a controlled ablation : six systems sharing one
backbone (microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base), one ngerprinted split, one frozen
label mapping, one tokenizer conguration and one seed, diering only in the
optimisation recipe and each scored once on the held-out test set. The chain
(Sect. 4) runs from a plain baseline, to a multi-view pooling head, to a variant
adding discriminative rates and class-weighted two-stage training, to a three-
stage curriculum.
      Our contributions are:


     A comparison of six optimisation recipes for three-class Vietnamese hate
      speech detection under one xed split and seed, reported with split nger-
      prints, per-class metrics, bootstrap intervals and paired signicance tests so
      it can be reproduced rather than re-argued.
     A multi-view classication head (rst-token, masked-mean and masked-max
      pooling) attaining the best macro-F1 at essentially the same parameter count
      as the standard head.
     An empirical demonstration that the recipes are not uniformly better: no
      pairwise macro-F1 dierence is signicant (a failure to detect, not proof
      of equivalence), the most accurate system has one of the lowest macro-
      F1 scores, a three-stage curriculum never wins validation selection, and a
      validation-searched threshold collapses to the trivial 0.5negative results
      we discuss for applied moderation.



2      Related Work
2.1     Vietnamese Hate Speech Detection
Hate speech detection is well studied for high-resource languages, but Vietnamese
resources appeared comparatively late. The UIT-ViHSD corpus [11] is the ref-
erence three-class dataset for Vietnamese social media; the VLSP 2019 shared
task [21] was an earlier benchmark, and related resources include UIT-ViCTSD
[14] and ViHOS for hate/oensive span detection [7]; the SMTCE benchmark
           SensitiveAI: Ablation of Optimization Recipes for Vietnamese HSD     3



[16] supplies the published numbers we reuse. Vietnamese-specic encoders have
since raised the state of the art: ViDeBERTa [19] is a monolingual DeBERTa
pre-trained on Vietnamese, ViSoBERT [15] adapts to social-media text and is, to
our knowledge, the strongest reported ViHSD encoder, ViHateT5 [17] reformu-
lates the task as text-to-text, and pipelines such as PhoBERT-CNN [20] report
higher scores with steps outside our controlled setting.



2.2     Pre-trained Encoders
Fine-tuning pre-trained transformers is the dominant approach, with PhoBERT
[13] for Vietnamese and mBERT [4] and XLM-R [2] for multilingual settings.
DeBERTa [6] introduced disentangled attention and DeBERTaV3 [5] replaced
masked-language modelling with replaced-token detection; the multilingual
checkpoint microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base, extending this to 100+ languages,
is the backbone used here.



2.3     Learning under Class Imbalance
ViHSD is strongly imbalanced and the minority classes are the main error source.
Common remedies re-weight the loss by training frequencythe eective number
of samples [3] or the inverse frequencyuse focal loss, which down-weights easy
examples [9], or move the decision threshold away from 0.5. Curriculum learning
[1] schedules diculty or label focus over time, and discriminative ne-tuning
with layer-wise rates was popularised by ULMFiT [8]. We x the encoder and
optimiser family and vary exactly these factors; most prior ViHSD work reports
a single aggregate improvement, so we deliberately surface the negative ndings
that single-number reports hide.



3      Dataset
3.1     UIT-ViHSD
We use UIT-ViHSD [11], a human-annotated corpus of Vietnamese social-media
comments, each carrying exactly one label: clean, offensive (insulting but not
hate speech) or hate (targeting a person or protected group).
      We follow the ocial train/dev/test partition. Preprocessing is deliberately
light and label-preserving: strip leading/trailing whitespace, collapse runs of
whitespace, drop control characters, and drop only rows with no usable text
(2 rows in train). We keep duplicates and do not lowercase, strip diacritics, nor-
malise teencode or truncate emoji, because those surface cues are part of the
signal. This yields 33,398 comments (24,046 / 2,672 / 6,680 for train/dev/test);
the corpus has 33,400 rowsthe dierence is exactly the 2 empty-text train rows
(Table 1). Comments are short and uneven (test mean 47.5 characters, median
33; hate about twice as long as the others). The annotation study reports only
moderate inter-annotator agreement (κ ≈ 0.5), which sets a ceiling on accuracy:
some test comments are genuinely ambiguous between offensive and hate,
and no recipe removes that label noise.

3.2     Label Mapping and Imbalance
The native ViHSD identiers are (0 = clean, 1 = offensive, 2 = hate); be-
cause the minority classes are the object of study, we freeze a canonical mapping
with the rare class rst (hate = 0, offensive = 1, clean = 2), applied once
and never changed.
      The distribution is skewed and stable across splits: clean is 8283% of every
split, hate about 10%, and offensive the smallest class at 6.67.9% (test: 688
hate, 444 offensive, 5,548 clean). Two consequences follow. First, accuracy
is misleadinga degenerate clean-always classier would score about 0.83 yet
be uselessso macro-F1 is primary. Second, oensive and hate speech share
most surface markers, so the interesting comparison is the trade-o between
minority-class F1 and the easy clean class.



3.3     Fixed Split and Reproducibility
The split must be identical for every system, so we store it as CSV les and
record each le's SHA-256 digest in a manifest copied into every run directory;
all six systems read the same three les and label mapping, leaving the recipe as
the only varying factor, with the test split scored once per system after hyper-
parameters are frozen on validation (Table 1).



3.4     Duplication Across Splits
Because we keep duplicates, we measured their cross-split prevalence. The ocial
ViHSD partition reuses comments: after case-folding, 895 of the 6,680 test com-
ments (13.4%) have an exact duplicate in train or validation and 118 duplicated
strings carry conicting labels, a property of the public split that can only atter
absolute scores. Table 2 re-scores all six systems on the 5,785 duplicate-free com-
ments: accuracy drops by 0.30.5 points for every system while macro-F1 and
its ordering barely move (M3 still highest at 0.6654; only the two lowest systems
swap, by 0.0003), so the conclusions do not rest on the overlap (Sect. 7.2).

4.4     Losses and Class Weighting
The    baseline   uses   unweighted   cross-entropy;   later   systems   use   weighted
cross-entropy (class weights = inverse square root of the training frequency,
normalised to mean one), focal loss  [9] with γ = 2 and αhate = 0.5,
αoffensive = αclean = 0.25 combined with those weights, or manual weights
hate:3, offensive:2, clean:1 for the curriculum's nal stage. Class weights
are computed on the training split only.



4.5     Discriminative Learning Rates and Two-Stage Training
Two optimisation ideas are combined in M3.


Discriminative (layer-wise) learning rates. Following ULMFiT [8], parameters
are split into four groups with decreasing rates from head to input (embeddings
5 × 10−6 , lower half 1 × 10−5 , upper half 2 × 10−5 , head 1 × 10−4 ), so the
         SensitiveAI: Ablation of Optimization Recipes for Vietnamese HSD           7



task-specic head adapts quickly from random initialisation while pre-trained
representations avoid catastrophic forgetting. The encoder starts from the same
checkpoint as M1 and M2, so the recipes share one initialisation.


Two-stage training. Training has two stages of up to 15 epochs each: stage 1
                                                                          −5
stabilises the representation under class-weighted cross-entropy at rate 2 × 10
with warmup 0.1; stage 2 continues from its checkpoint with lower rates and
focal loss, which down-weights easy examples. Here the selected checkpoint was
the end of stage 1 (validation macro-F1 0.7089); focal stage 2 never improved on
it and stopped early, so M3's headline number comes from discriminative rates
plus class-weighted cross-entropy, not focal loss.


Label-specic decision thresholds. The decision rule is changed from argmax to
per-class thresholds: a label is accepted when its probability reaches its own
threshold, the highest-probability accepted label wins, and if none passes the
plain argmax is used. The thresholds are searched to maximise validation macro-
F1 by coordinate ascent over a 101-point grid in [0, 1] from 0.5, on the validation
split only, then frozen and applied unchanged to the test pass.



4.6   Three-Stage Curriculum Concept Training
M4 replaces the two stages with a three-stage curriculum [1] that shapes the
representation with easier binary sub-problems before the nal decision: stage 1
targets  offensive versus anything else, stage 2             hate versus anything
else, and stage 3       returns to the three-class objective with class weights
hate:3, offensive:2, clean:1. The full dataset is used in every stage (a
concept's negative class is the other two classes) and the three-way head is
kept throughout, the concept loss simply re-reading the three logits as a binary
                                                                                   −5
objective. Each stage runs up to 10 epochs with rates decreasing from 2 × 10
            −5                                           −5
to 1 × 10        (stage 3 raises this slightly to 1.5 × 10    ) and the discriminative
grouping of Sect. 4.5. M4 asks whether a ner sequence of increasingly dicult
stages beats M3's two. All three stages run, but no epoch after the rst improves
the validation macro-F1 used for checkpoint selection (best 0.694), so the ner
sequence does not win.

5.2    Training Protocol


All systems share one conguration: AdamW [10] with weight decay 0.01, a lin-
ear schedule with warmup 0.050.1, batch size 16 with gradient accumulation 2
(eective 32), gradient clipping at norm 1.0, sequence length 256 tokens, bf16
training (fp32 for the latency benchmark), and up to 1530 epochs depending
on the recipe. Early stopping monitors validation macro-F1 with patience 6 and
restores the best checkpoint. The systems dier only in the encoder initialisa-
tion, the number of stages, the loss sequence and the class-weighting scheme
(Sect. 4.2); the test split is evaluated exactly once per system with the check-
point selected on validation. Table 3 lists the resolved conguration and the
frozen checkpoint (best epoch and its validation macro-F1) for each system. Ev-
ery system reads the same three split les (veried by SHA-256 digest) and label
mapping, and all class weights and thresholds are estimated on training and val-
idation only, never on test; only the head, learning rates, number of stages and
loss vary.

6      Results
6.1      Headline Comparison
Table 4 reports the test results. The systems are tightly clusteredmacro-F1
spans 0.65360.6638 (1.0 pt) and accuracy 0.86330.8783and these spreads
sit inside sampling noise: the 95% bootstrap intervals on macro-F1 are about
±0.019 wide (Table 5), so every pairwise gap lies within a single interval and
none is signicant (Sect. 6.6). The observations below are directions of eect,
not established rankings.
      The full recipe (M3) is best by macro-F1 (0.6638) and OFFENSIVE F1
(0.4561); the extended probe (M5) has the highest accuracy (0.8783) and
CLEAN F1 yet the lowest macro ROC-AUC (0.8096); the stage-2 probe (M6)
has the highest HATE F1 (0.6088), ROC-AUC (0.8930) and AP, but the
lowest accuracy; and the custom head alone moves macro-F1 only to 0.6575.



6.2      Ablation Reading
Table 4 gives the chain: each step swaps a whole recipe, so a gap is the combined
eect of its changes, not a single-factor estimate. The custom head adds +0.30
macro-F1 (M1→M2); discriminative rates, two-stage class-weighted training and
a threshold search another +0.63 (M2→M3); and the curriculum gives back 0.48
(M3→M4). The two probes are most informative: class-weighted focal loss on a
standard head (M6) reaches 0.6604, beating the baseline, the custom head and
the curriculum at the best HATE F1 and ranking quality; extending stage 1 (M5)
hurts (macro-F1 0.6536, ROC-AUC 0.8096), evidence that further cross-entropy
training drifts to the majority class. M3's selected checkpoint was the end of its
cross-entropy stage (val. macro-F1 0.7089 at epoch 15), so its focal stage never
helped: the same ingredient succeeds or fails depending on what precedes it.

Because the spread is so small we re-analyse the frozen predictions with a per-

sided McNemar test for correctness; the two tests answer dierent questions
and are reported separately. Table 5 gives the intervals, the top-label expected
calibration error (ECE) and the paired comparisons.
   Two conclusions follow. First, no pairwise macro-F1 dierence is sig-
nicant: the largest gap (M3−M5, +0.010) has paired interval [−0.002, +0.023]
containing zero (p = 0.11). Second, the accuracy ranking is more stable,
and there the McNemar test does reject: M6 is signicantly less accurate
than both M3 and M1 (p < 0.001 each), because it trades minority recall for
majority predictionsthe one paired dierence in the whole study that survives
correction. Calibration also diers (focal-only probe best, ECE 0.068; full recipe
worst, 0.120), so the defensible claim is not M3 is best but the recipes tie
on macro-F1, and the choice should rest on secondary criteria (minority recall,
calibration, cost).

7      Discussion
7.1    Which Decisions Actually Matter
All recipes sit within 1.0 macro-F1 point and, as Sect. 6.6 shows, these gaps are
not statistically distinguishable under our tests (not proof of equivalence), so
the ablation gives only the direction of the eects. Class-weighted focal loss on
a plain head (M6) reached 0.6604, matching the full recipe within noise while
giving the best minority recall and macro ROC-AUC (its macro AP is second
to M1's), so the loss change gave the largest observed shift; the multi-view head
           SensitiveAI: Ablation of Optimization Recipes for Vietnamese HSD     13



alone adds only +0.30 macro-F1. More stages did not help: both multi-stage
systems were frozen at a stage-1 checkpoint, so the extra stages never improved
validation selection. The threshold search collapsed to 0.5 (M3, M5, M6) or failed
to transfer (M4), so the decision rule added no gain.
      The accuracy leader is also the macro-F1 laggard (Sect. 6.3), a direct conse-
quence of an 83%-majority test set: each extra clean prediction raises accuracy
even when it misclassies a minority comment, so an accuracy-optimised system
would under-report oensive and hateful contentthe opposite of the product
requirement. Selection should therefore use macro-F1 (or minority recall), driven
by validation so the test split stays honest.



7.2     Threats to Validity
Each system was trained once (seed 42), so the estimates carry run-to-run vari-
ance the shared split and seed do not remove; the bootstrap intervals quantify
sampling noise but not training noise, and as the observed gaps already lie in-
side one interval we claim no recipe is reliably better. The ocial split reuses
comments across train/dev/test (Sect. 3); we report this rather than hide it,
and the de-duplicated check leaves the macro-F1 ordering eectively unchanged
(Table 2), but absolute scores are optimistic under a strictly leakage-free proto-
col. ViHSD is the only corpus, its external baselines (Table 6) reprint published
numbers under other settings, and the latency runs were inconsistent between
identical architectures, so no cost claim is made. Agreement is only moderate
(κ ≈ 0.5), so part of the error is irreducible.



7.3     Implications
For applied moderation the message is negative but worth reporting: no single
trick solves Vietnamese hate speech detection, and added complexitycurricula,
searched thresholds, longer trainingdid not pay o. The minority classes re-
main hard (OFFENSIVE F1 stuck near 0.46 across all six systems), which is
consistent with the labelled data, not the optimiser, being the binding con-
straint; more labelled oensive examples or span-level supervision as in ViHOS
[7] are plausible next steps.



8      Conclusion
We presented SensitiveAI, a controlled comparison of six optimisation recipes for
three-class Vietnamese hate speech detection on UIT-ViHSD. Holding the split,
backbone, tokenizer and seed xed while swapping whole recipeshead, losses,
rates, stages and decision rule togetherlets us read the chain as the combined
direction of each design choice. Our maindeliberately modestnding is that
we detected no macro-F1 dierence among the recipes: the 1.0-point spread lies
within one paired-bootstrap interval and no pairwise dierence is signicant.
This is a failure to detect, not proof of equivalence. The only robust paired

dierences are in accuracy : the focal-only probe (M6), which reaches the highest
minority recall by giving up clean recall, is signicantly less accurate than M3
and M1 (McNemar          p < 0.001, both surviving a HolmBonferroni correction
over the seven reported comparisons), so the highest-accuracy system (M5) is
also the macro-F1 laggardaccuracy is unsafe on an 83%-majority corpus. The
best macro-F1 (0.6638, M3) edges the baseline by under a point; M6 gives the
best HATE F1 (0.6088) and macro ROC-AUC (0.8930) at a slightly lower macro-
F1 (0.6604); the custom head alone adds only +0.30. Multi-stage training never
won on validationboth multi-stage systems froze a stage-1 checkpoint, so the
three-stage curriculum added cost but no selected gain. OFFENSIVE F1 stays
near 0.46 for every recipe, consistent with the labelled data, not the optimiser,
being the binding constraint.


Future Work. Repeat each system with several seeds to quantify training vari-
ance and test the gaps against it; re-train the published baselines under the
same split so Table 6 becomes a controlled comparison; and enrich the data
(span-level supervision, hard-negative retrieval, targeted offensive examples).
A controlled latency study is still needed before any throughput claim about the
custom head.
