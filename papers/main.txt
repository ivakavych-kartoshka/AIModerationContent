      Abstract. Hate speech on Vietnamese social media is a pressing
      moderation problem, and ne-tuned multilingual transformers such as
      mDeBERTa-v3 are now a common approach. Reported gains, however,
      usually bundle several independent design decisionsclassication head,
      learning-rate schedule, loss function, class weighting, training curriculum
      and decision ruleinto one number, so it is unclear which decision
      actually matters. We present SensitiveAI, a controlled ablation of six
      such systems that share one xed data split, one frozen label mapping,
      one backbone (microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base) and one random seed,
      and dier only in the optimisation recipe. Every system is trained
      on UIT-ViHSD (three classes HATE / OFFENSIVE / CLEAN, 83%
      CLEAN) and scored exactly once on the held-out test set. All six
      systems land in a narrow band of 0.6540.664 macro-F1 and 0.8630.878
      accuracy, and no pairwise macro-F1 dierence is signicant under a
      paired bootstrap or an exact McNemar test. A multi-view classication
      head with discriminative learning rates reaches the best macro-F1
      (0.6638), a focal-only continuation of the plain baseline gives the best
      HATE F1 (0.609) and macro ROC-AUC (0.893), and the highest
      accuracy (0.878) belongs to the system with the lowest macro-F1the
      classic imbalanced-classication trap. A three-stage curriculum and
      a validation-searched threshold rule did not improve over simpler
      recipes. We report the full protocol, bootstrap condence intervals, a
      confusion-matrix and error analysis, and the negative results, so the
      comparison can be reproduced rather than re-argued.

      Keywords: hate speech detection · Vietnamese NLP · mDeBERTa ·
      class imbalance · ablation study · reproducibility.


1   Introduction
Vietnam has one of the fastest-growing social-media user bases in Southeast
Asia, and the volume of Vietnamese text that platforms must moderate keeps
rising. Automatically agging hateful and oensive comments is therefore a prac-
tical prerequisite for healthier online communities, yet Vietnamese remains low-
resource, and its social-media style is hardcode-switching, teencode, informal

spellings, character substitutions used to evade lters, and frequently dropped
diacritics.
      The release of   UIT-ViHSD [11], a human-annotated corpus of roughly 33k
Vietnamese comments labelled clean, offensive or hate, made supervised
transformer ne-tuning a realistic default, and the dataset is now a standard
benchmark for monolingual encoders such as PhoBERT [13] and multilingual
encoders such as mBERT and XLM-R [2,16,7]. Multilingual DeBERTa-v3 vari-
ants (mDeBERTa-v3) have recently become popular because they combine a large
multilingual pre-training corpus with disentangled attention [6,5].
      In parallel, many   optimisation tricks are routinely applied to imbalanced
text classication: a richer classication head, discriminative (layer-wise) learn-
ing rates, multi-stage ne-tuning, class-weighted or focal losses [9], curriculum
learning [1], and per-class decision thresholds [18]. In the literature these are usu-
ally bundled together and reported as a single end-to-end improvement, which

    This paper answers that with a controlled ablation. We build six systems

      Our contributions are:

     A comparison of six optimisation recipes for three-class Vietnamese hate
      speech detection under a single xed split and seed, reported with split
      ngerprints, per-class metrics, bootstrap condence intervals and paired sig-

     A multi-view classication head (rst-token, masked-mean and masked-max

2      Related Work
2.1     Vietnamese Hate Speech Detection
Hate speech detection has been studied extensively for high-resource languages,
but Vietnamese resources appeared comparatively late. The       UIT-ViHSD corpus
            SensitiveAI: Ablation of Optimization Recipes for Vietnamese HSD        3

[11] is the reference dataset for three-class classication on Vietnamese social me-
dia; the VLSP 2019 shared task [21] provided an earlier, smaller benchmark, and
related resources include UIT-ViCTSD [14] and ViHOS for hate/oensive span
detection [7]. The SMTCE benchmark [16] re-evaluates BERTology models on
ViHSD and supplies the published numbers we use in Sect. 6. Vietnamese-specic
encoders have since pushed the number higher: ViDeBERTa [19] is a monolin-
gual DeBERTa pre-trained on a large Vietnamese corpus, ViSoBERT [15] adapts
to social-media text and is, to our knowledge, the strongest reported ViHSD en-
coder, and ViHateT5 [17] reformulates the task as text-to-text. Augmentation
pipelines such as PhoBERT-CNN [20] also report higher scores, though with
pre-processing and synthetic-data steps outside our controlled setting.



2.2     Pre-trained Encoders
Fine-tuning pre-trained transformers is the dominant approach. Monolingual
Vietnamese encoders are represented by PhoBERT [13]; multilingual encoders
by mBERT [4] and XLM-R [2]. DeBERTa [6] introduced disentangled attention
and    an   enhanced   mask   decoder,   and   DeBERTaV3     [5]   replaced   masked-
language modelling with replaced-token detection in the ELECTRA style,
improving accuracy and pre-training eciency. The multilingual checkpoint
microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base extends this to 100+ languages and is the
backbone used here.



2.3     Learning under Class Imbalance
ViHSD is strongly imbalanced, and the minority classes are the main source
of error. Common remedies re-weight the loss by the training frequencythe
eective number of samples [3] or the inverse frequencyuse focal loss, which
down-weights easy examples [9], or move the decision threshold away from 0.5.
Curriculum learning [1] schedules the diculty or label focus over time, and
discriminative ne-tuning with layer-wise rates was popularised by ULMFiT
[8]. We keep the encoder and optimiser family xed and vary exactly these
factors; most prior ViHSD papers instead report a single aggregate improvement
from several changes at once, so our contribution is complementary, deliberately
surfacing negative ndings that single-number reports hide.



3      Dataset
3.1     UIT-ViHSD
We use    UIT-ViHSD [11], a human-annotated corpus of Vietnamese social-media
comments. Each comment carries exactly one label: clean (no hate or oensive
content), offensive (insulting language that does not rise to hate speech), or
hate (hate speech targeting a person or a protected group).
      We follow the ocial train/dev/test partition. Our preprocessing is delib-
erately light and label-preserving: we strip leading/trailing whitespace, collapse
4         F. Author et al.

runs of whitespace, drop control characters and remove exact duplicate (text, la-
bel) pairs; we do not lowercase, remove diacritics, normalise teencode or truncate
emoji, because those surface cues are part of the signal the models must learn.
This yields 33,398 usable comments (24,046 / 2,672 / 6,680 for train/dev/test);
the corpus has 33,400 rows and the small dierence is duplicate removal. The
comments are short and uneven: the test split is 47.5 characters on average (me-
dian 33), and hate comments are about twice as long (mean 84) as offensive
or clean ones (mean 44 and 43). The annotation study reports only moderate
inter-annotator agreement (around 0.5), which sets a ceiling on achievable ac-
curacy: some test comments are genuinely ambiguous between offensive and
hate, and no optimisation recipe can remove that label noise.


3.2     Label Mapping and Imbalance
The native ViHSD identiers are (0 = clean, 1 = offensive, 2 = hate); be-
cause the minority classes are the object of study, we freeze a canonical mapping
with the rare class rst (hate = 0, offensive = 1, clean = 2), applied once
and never changed.
      The distribution is skewed and stable across splits: clean accounts for 82
83% of every split, hate for about 10%, and offensive is the smallest class
at 6.67.9% (test: 688 hate, 444 offensive, 5,548 clean). Two consequences
drive the rest of the paper. First, accuracy is misleading: a degenerate classier
predicting clean always would score about       0.83 yet be useless, so we treat
macro-F1 as primary alongside per-class precision, recall and F1. Second, the
classes are confusable by designoensive and hate speech share most surface
markersso the interesting comparison is the trade-o between minority-class
F1 and the easy clean class.



3.3     Fixed Split and Reproducibility
The split must be identical for every system, so we store it as CSV les and
record each le's SHA-256 digest in a manifest copied into every run directory;
all six systems read the same three les and share the label mapping, leaving
the recipe as the only varying factor, with the test split scored exactly once per
system after all hyper-parameters are frozen on validation.

4.4     Losses and Class Weighting

The baseline uses unweighted cross-entropy; later systems use one of three vari-
ants:   weighted cross-entropy with class weights equal to the inverse square root
of the training frequency (normalised to mean one);      focal loss [9] with γ = 2
and label-specic weights αhate       = 0.5, αoffensive = αclean = 0.25, combined
with the class weights; or      manual weights hate:3, offensive:2, clean:1 for
the curriculum's nal stage. Class weights are always computed on the training
split only.




4.5     Discriminative Learning Rates and Two-Stage Training

Two optimisation ideas are combined in M3.
          SensitiveAI: Ablation of Optimization Recipes for Vietnamese HSD              7

Discriminative (layer-wise) learning rates. Following ULMFiT [8], parameters
are split into four groups with decreasing rates from head to input: embeddings
5×10−6 , lower transformer half 1×10−5 , upper half 2×10−5 , and head 1×10−4 ,
so the task-specic head adapts quickly from random initialisation while pre-
trained representations avoid catastrophic forgetting. The encoder starts from
the same mdeberta-v3-base checkpoint as M1 and M2, so the recipes share one
initialisation.


Two-stage training. Training proceeds in two stages of up to 15 epochs each:
stage 1 stabilises the representation under class-weighted cross-entropy at rate
2 × 10−5 with warmup 0.1; stage 2 continues from the stage-1 checkpoint with
lower discriminative rates and focal loss, so hard and rare examples dominate the
gradient. In this run the selected checkpoint was the     end of stage 1 (validation
macro-F1 0.7089); the focal stage 2 never improved on it and was stopped early,
so M3's headline number in practice comes from discriminative rates plus class-
weighted cross-entropy, not from focal loss.


Label-specic decision thresholds. The decision rule is changed from argmax to
per-class thresholds: a label is accepted when its probability reaches its own
threshold, the highest-probability accepted label wins, and if none passes the
plain argmax is used. The thresholds are searched to maximise validation macro-
F1 by coordinate ascent over a 101-point grid in [0, 1] from 0.5, on the validation
split only, then frozen and applied unchanged to the test pass.



4.6   Three-Stage Curriculum Concept Training
M4 replaces the two stages with a three-stage curriculum inspired by curriculum
learning [1], shaping the representation with easier binary sub-problems before
the nal three-way decision: stage 1 targets  offensive versus anything else,
stage 2  hate versus anything else, and stage 3 returns to the three-class objec-
tive with class weights hate:3, offensive:2, clean:1 and cross-entropy. Two
choices keep this a genuine curriculum: the full dataset is used in    every stage (a
concept's negative class is the other two classes), and the three-way head is kept
throughout, the concept loss just re-reading the three logits as a binary objective.

5     Experimental Setup
5.1   Hardware and Software
All experiments were run on a single workstation with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX
4060 Laptop GPU (8 GB), CUDA 12.6, PyTorch 2.13 and Python 3.11, using the
8         F. Author et al.

Hugging Face Transformers library [22]. The backbone has 278.8M parameters;
the custom head adds about 594k (≈ 0.2%), so the systems are of essentially
identical size.



5.2     Training Protocol
All systems share one conguration: AdamW [10] with weight decay 0.01, a lin-
ear schedule with warmup 0.050.1, batch size 16 with gradient accumulation 2
(eective 32), gradient clipping at norm 1.0, sequence length 256 tokens, bf16
training (fp32 for the latency benchmark), and up to 1530 epochs depending
on the recipe. Early stopping monitors validation macro-F1 with patience 6 and
restores the best checkpoint. The systems dier only in the encoder initialisa-
tion, the number of stages, the loss sequence and the class-weighting scheme
(Sect. 4.2); the test split is evaluated exactly once per system with the check-
point selected on validation.



5.3     Metrics
Because of the imbalance described in Sect. 3.2, we treat   macro-averaged F1 as
the primary metric and report accuracy only as a secondary, diagnostic number,
alongside per-class precision, recall and F1, the macro ROC-AUC and the macro
average precision (AP), since precision-recall summaries are more trustworthy
than ROC under imbalance [18]. Calibration uses the top-label expected calibra-
tion error (ECE, 10 bins); signicance uses a percentile bootstrap and the exact
McNemar test. Inference cost is reported as milliseconds per sample on the same
GPU, as an indicative measurement only.



5.4     Fairness of the Comparison
Every system reads the same three split les (veried by SHA-256 digest) and
label mapping, uses the same encoder checkpoint family and seed, and is scored
on the same test set; class weights and thresholds, when used, are estimated on
the training and validation splits respectively, never on the test set. The only
things that vary are the head, the learning rates, the number of stages and the
loss.

6.3   Accuracy Versus Macro-F1
The clearest lesson is the accuracy/macro-F1 contrast: the most accurate sys-
tem (M5, 0.8783) is the one    not to deployit has the worst OFFENSIVE re-
call (0.381) and the weakest ranking quality, because predicting the majority
class is rewarded by accuracy and punished by macro-F1. We therefore advise
against accuracy-based selection here, consistent with the classical preference for
precision-recall over ROC analysis under imbalance [18].



6.4   Training Dynamics
Validation accuracy plateaus within about ve epochs in a narrow 0.860.88
band, so most dierences are settled early; the selected checkpoints fall at dier-
ent epochs (9 for M1, 15 for M3, 3 for M5), so the dynamics, not the nal epoch,
decide each score. Because validation loss turns upward while training loss keeps
fallingand systems that change objective between stages show a step in the
curveearly stopping on validation macro-F1, not loss, selects the checkpoint.

requirement. Selection should therefore use macro-F1 (or minority recall), driven
by the validation split so the test split stays honest.



7.2     Threats to Validity
Each system was trained once (seed 42), so the point estimates carry run-to-
run variance that a shared split and seed do not remove; the bootstrap intervals
quantify sampling noise but not training noise, and because the observed gaps al-
ready lie inside one interval we cannot claim any recipe is reliably better. ViHSD
is the only corpus studied, and its external baselines (Table 3) reprint published
numbers under other settings and are indicative, not controlled. The latency
measurements were single-session and inconsistent between identical architec-
tures, so no cost claim is made. Finally, ViHSD's inter-annotator agreement is
only moderate (κ ≈ 0.5), so part of the remaining error is irreducible.



7.3     Implications
For applied moderation the message is negative but worth reporting: no single
trick solves Vietnamese hate speech detection, and added complexitycurricula,

at the data rather than the optimiser as the next place to invest: more labelled

8    Conclusion
We presented SensitiveAI, a controlled ablation of six optimisation recipes for
three-class Vietnamese hate speech detection on UIT-ViHSD. Holding the data
split, label mapping, backbone, tokenizer and seed xed while changing one
recipe at a time let us isolate the direction of each design choice. Our mainand
deliberately modestnding is that the recipes are     statistically indistinguishable
on macro-F1: the 1.0-point spread lies within one bootstrap interval and no pair-
wise McNemar comparison is signicant. The only robust signal is on accuracy,
where the majority-heavy probe is signicantly less accurate than the balanced
recipes; the highest-accuracy system is thus also the macro-F1 laggard, conrm-
ing that accuracy is an unsafe selection criterion on an 83%-majority corpus.
The best macro-F1 (0.6604, from focal loss on a plain head, M6) barely exceeds
the baseline, the full recipe (M3) reaches 0.6638, and the custom head alone
gives only +0.30 points: the blocker is the data and the task, not the optimiser,
since OFFENSIVE F1 stays near 0.46 for every system.


Future Work. Repeat each system with several seeds to quantify training vari-
ance and test the gaps against it; re-train the published baselines under the same
split to make Table 3 a controlled comparison; and attack the data rather than
the optimiser, since span-level supervision, hard-negative retrieval and targeted
collection of offensive examples look more promising. A controlled latency
study is also needed before any throughput claim about the custom head.
